Grovia

Autonomous plant watering system, built from a bare component list to a working dashboard
OBJECTIVE

Design and build a plant watering system that runs on its own, from hardware selection through firmware and a dashboard, without a phone app or a cloud service to depend on.

REQUIREMENTS
  • Water based on measured soil moisture, not a fixed timer
  • Keep working if WiFi drops
  • Store historical moisture and temperature data locally
  • Show usable 24-hour, 7-day, and 30-day charts on a live dashboard
PROCESS
  • Chose an ESP32 for onboard WiFi and enough compute to run a web server alongside sensor sampling.
  • Sensor and driver set: DHT11 for temperature and humidity, a capacitive soil moisture sensor, and an L298N driver for the pump.
  • Built firmware through multiple versions, adding WiFi-independent operation, offline notification queuing, a physical boot button, and LittleFS for storage on the device itself.
  • Raw moisture readings were noisy. Tuned exponential smoothing (alpha 0.6) and calibrated dry and wet reference points against measured sensor output to get a stable reading.
  • Replaced simple on/off watering with a pulse-irrigation algorithm: once soil crosses the dry threshold, the system pulses water toward a 70% moisture target, waits 3 minutes between pulses to let water absorb, and sizes each following pulse, capped at 30 seconds, from how much the last pulse raised moisture.
  • Found the 7-day and 30-day charts were only showing the current day's data. Traced it to how chart data was buffered and fixed it with an hourly buffer spanning the full period.
RESULTS
  • Runs unattended and waters based on actual soil condition instead of a schedule.
  • Firmware at v3.0, with a morning briefing and next-water prediction on the dashboard.

Self-balancing robot

Two-wheeled robot, balanced with IMU sensor fusion and a tuned PID loop — balances indefinitely
OBJECTIVE

Build a two-wheeled robot that balances itself using IMU feedback and motor control, as a hands-on way to learn control theory beyond coursework.

REQUIREMENTS
  • Read orientation fast enough to correct before the robot falls
  • Drive both motors independently based on tilt angle
  • Recover from a push without falling over
  • Accept remote commands (drive, turn, stop) without breaking the balance loop
  • Avoid driving into obstacles while balancing
PROCESS
  • Learned Arduino from the ground up with an Elegoo Mega R3 starter kit, working through smaller projects before this one.
  • Built the robot on an Arduino Mega with an MPU6500 IMU and an L298N motor driver.
  • Started with a complementary filter for orientation. It lagged and drifted under fast tilts, which threw off the balance response.
  • Switched to a Madgwick filter, which cut lag and drift and gave the balance controller cleaner input to work from.
  • Tuned a PID loop on top of the filtered angle: Kp = 12.0, Ki = 300.0, Kd = 1.05, running on a 10 ms loop. Integral is clamped to ±5 to stop windup, and the derivative term is low-pass filtered (70/30 blend) to cut noise from raw angle-rate spikes.
  • Balance point is a single tunable setpoint (0.75°) rather than hardcoded into the control math, so re-leveling the robot after a rebuild is a one-line change.
  • Added an IR remote (NEC protocol): forward/back briefly nudge the setpoint off balance to lean the robot into motion, left/right apply a turn bias between the two wheels, and a dedicated button triggers an immediate motor cutoff independent of the balance loop.
  • Added an ultrasonic sensor for basic obstacle avoidance: inside 30 cm the robot nudges its setpoint backward to back away, then returns to upright once clear.
  • Added a hard safety cutoff: if the filtered angle ever exceeds 30° from setpoint, the robot assumes it's fallen and kills both motors rather than fighting a lost cause.
RESULTS
  • Balances indefinitely on two wheels under normal conditions, correcting continuously off the Madgwick-filtered angle.
  • Recovers from pushes within the PID's operating range without falling, and cuts power cleanly if it exceeds the 30° fall threshold instead of thrashing the motors.
  • Drives, turns, and stops on command from the IR remote while still balancing, and backs away from obstacles inside 30 cm on its own.

Rotary position control & Furuta pendulum stabilization

Systems & Dynamics Controls, Quanser Qube-Servo 3 — from PD position control of a rotary disk to full-state feedback stabilization of an inverted pendulum
OBJECTIVE

Two linked projects on the same hardware. Midterm: model the Qube-Servo 3 DC motor and design a PD controller to hold the rotary arm at a commanded angular position. Final: attach a Furuta pendulum to that same arm and design a controller to stabilize it upright (θ = 0) using motor voltage as the only input.

REQUIREMENTS
  • Midterm: position step response with ≤5% overshoot and settling time under 0.15 s
  • Model had to match manufacturer specs and measured 6V step data before it could be trusted for controller design
  • Final: stabilize the pendulum at upright from a small initial disturbance, motor voltage limited to ±10V
  • Controller had to be validated on a provided hardware-simulation p-code (run_Quanser_cube.p), not just on the analytical model
PROCESS
  • Motor modeling. Derived a DC motor + inertia disk model both with and without motor inductance. Fit the unknown viscous damping term (bθ) by back-calculating it from measured 6V step data, since it wasn't on the datasheet. Result: bθ = 4.735×10-6 N·m·s/rad, giving a no-load speed prediction of 5431 RPM against a manufacturer spec of 5400 RPM (0.6% error) and a mechanical time constant of 75.4 ms against a measured 80 ms (5.7% error, attributed to static friction and the amplifier's 0.65V deadband).
  • Compared the eigenvalues of both models: the electrical pole (−6508.7 rad/s) is about 490× faster than the mechanical pole (−13.3 rad/s), which justified dropping inductance from the position-control model without meaningfully changing the response.
  • PD position control. Targeted 5% overshoot and a settling time under 0.15 s: solved for ζ = 0.6901, chose ωn = 42 rad/s for margin under the limit, and got Kp = 5.7367, Kd = 0.1455. Forward-Euler simulation matched the design almost exactly (5.0% predicted vs. 5.0% simulated overshoot; 138 ms predicted vs. 142.6 ms simulated settling time). Tested on the hardware p-code at a 2.0 rad reference and landed within 0.33° of target.
  • Pendulum modeling. For the final project, derived the full nonlinear equations of motion for the arm + pendulum system with Lagrangian mechanics (two generalized coordinates: arm angle φ, pendulum angle θ), using a thin-rod inertia approximation. Linearized about the upright equilibrium and reduced it to state-space form, then pulled the transfer functions numerically in MATLAB:

    Hφ(s) = (17.057s² + 36.607s − 1111.81) / (s⁴ + 6.381s³ − 71.816s² − 244.515s)
    Hθ(s) = −9.633s² / (s⁴ + 6.381s³ − 71.816s² − 244.515s)

    Open-loop eigenvalues: −10.909, −2.984, 0, +7.512 — the positive real pole confirms the upright position is mathematically unstable, which matches the physical pendulum falling on its own.
  • Controller choice. A single PID loop can only regulate one output, but this system has two coupled degrees of freedom (φ and θ) driven by one voltage input — it's underactuated. Chose full-state feedback (V = −Kx) instead, since it uses all four states (φ, θ, φ̇, θ̇) to compute the single input.
  • Checked controllability (rank of the controllability matrix = 4, confirming the input can influence all four states), then placed closed-loop poles with Ackermann's formula (MATLAB's place). First attempt at [−10, −11, −12, −13] pushed the commanded voltage past the ±10V amplifier limit, so the poles were relaxed to [−8, −9, −10, −11], which still met the 0.5 s settle-time target and gave a gain matrix K = [−7.1235, −89.3047, −3.4964, −9.4728]. The large gain on θ makes sense physically — it's the state the controller has to correct hardest.
  • Underactuation problem. Simple pole placement stabilized θ but left φ to drift freely, since nothing was driving the arm angle back to zero. Solved it by augmenting the state vector with the integral of the arm-angle error (a 5th state), which forces φ → 0 at steady state while still holding θ = 0.
RESULTS
  • Linear and nonlinear simulations of the pendulum controller agreed closely near θ = 0, confirming the linearization was valid in the operating region.
  • Closed-loop pole placement stabilized the pendulum from a 5° disturbance with all four closed-loop eigenvalues at −8, −9, −10, −11 as designed; the augmented integral-action version drove both φ and θ to zero while staying inside the ±10V voltage limit.
  • On the hardware p-code, the controller diverged faster than the linear/nonlinear models predicted and tripped the safety cutoff around 1 s. Traced this to hardware dynamics the model doesn't capture — encoder quantization noise, friction, and unmodeled nonlinearities — rather than a design error, since the same gains stabilized the system cleanly in both simulations.
  • PD position control (midterm) reached 0.33° steady-state error against a 2.0 rad commanded position on the hardware simulation.

Purely mechanical pachinko machine

MECH 3200 final design report, Team 51, Auburn University. A fully playable pachinko machine with no electronics, only mechanical linkages and gravity.
OBJECTIVE

Design and build a fully mechanical pachinko machine that cycles, launches, scores, and resets 20 steel ball bearings, using fabrication methods the team already had experience with instead of reaching for water jetting or welding.

TEAM

MECH 3200, Team 51: Drew Petro, Will Upton, Braedon Baker, John Hinton Hogue, Alex Shepherd.

GAME DESIGN
  • Strategy-based scoring. The ball can travel up a left or right path, each leading to different scoring options, so the player weighs a safer route against a higher-risk, higher-reward one.
  • Left path: a 25-point hole or a jackpot hole that releases several balls back into the launch zone. Right path: a 100-point hole or a gutter.
  • A 15° inclined connector sits before each path, so entering a path does not guarantee which scoring option the ball reaches.
  • Both paths feed a jump ramp, where the ball loops around and gains enough speed to clear a 1″ gap, then lands on a track switcher that alternates which way it sends each ball. This is what separates 25-point from jackpot, and 100-point from gutter.
  • Scored and jackpotted balls travel through clear tubing into a scoring chamber sized to hold all 20 balls on each side, so every scored ball releases at the same time on reset. That simultaneous release was a deliberate anti-cheating measure.
PROCESS
  • Chose fabrication methods around the team's existing strengths. Strong woodworking experience made MDF the natural choice for the frame, keeping it stable while minimizing weight.
  • One team member's SolidWorks experience made 3D printing the obvious way to build the playfield. The whole playfield was 3D printed, which allowed tight tolerances and geometric accuracy that would have been risky to cut by hand, and let the team iterate quickly while the game design was still changing.
  • No one started with metalworking experience, but the launcher frame and handle needed it, since those parts see the most repeated wear. The team picked it up through Auburn's makerspace instead of designing around the gap.
  • Sized the launcher spring from an impulse-momentum calculation: the ball needs 8.02 ft/s to clear the top of the playfield, which works out to a spring constant of 19.52 lbf/ft at a 1.5″ deflection. The team used a slightly stiffer spring, about 30 lbf/ft, to guarantee the ball reached full height on every launch.
  • Ran an FMEA before manufacturing and used it to catch two failure modes early: a launcher handle that could strip its threaded connection, and a track switcher with too much mass to reset reliably.
  • Two failures surfaced during manufacturing that the FMEA missed. The launcher body cracked at the base after repeated launches, so the team machined it from aluminum on a mill instead of thickening the plastic wall, which would have limited spring compression and launch force. Balls also stuck in the scoring tubes from too little slope, so the team redrilled the back frame with angled holes so gravity carried every ball into the score box.
  • The jackpot lever also failed to transfer enough energy to release stored balls. The fix was a lever with a counterweight and a curved contact face: the counterweight added the mass and strength needed to hold multiple balls, and the curve lengthened contact time with the scoring ball for a greater impulse.
  • During final testing, the doser released balls on its own. Angling the loading chute upslope so the ball sat back against the doser fixed it. Scoring balls were also missing the tubing entirely at times, which reorienting the scoring holes solved by letting gravity do the work.
RESULTS
  • Passed the proving trial: fired all 20 balls plus every surplus ball earned through jackpots, with scored balls rolling cleanly to the scoring area and dropping to the collection area together on reset.
  • Final device weighs under 11 lbs and cost $56.26 against a $100 budget, using five fabrication methods: 3D printing, woodworking, metalworking, hand tooling, and mechanical fastening.
  • Remaining weak point: nothing holds the balls waiting to load, so they can stack up or release early if the player is not careful.
